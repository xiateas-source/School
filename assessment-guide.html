What these are for

These four quests are here to help you plan, not to grade. They're meant to show which Grade 2 skills are secure, which just need a light review, whether Grade 3 work is pitched about right, what can be moved through quickly, and where a little parent-supported teaching would help.

They are not diagnostic evaluations or formal standardized tests, and they aren't professionally validated. Think of each one as a friendly conversation that happens to be written down — one useful source of information among many, alongside everything you already know about your child.

A note on tone. Your child never sees a score. The lantern cat simply travels a little further with each answer and reaches camp at the end. There is no pass or fail, and nothing here is designed to make a seven-year-old feel “behind.”

A suggested order

You can do the quests in any order, and you never have to do all four. If you'd like a default, this one works well:

  1. Reading Quest
  2. Math Quest
  3. Word Quest (language arts)
  4. Science Quest

Reading and math carry the most weight for placement, so they come first. Word/language arts builds on reading. Science is last because it depends least on a strict skill ladder — Grade 3 science moves to new topics rather than stacking on Grade 2, so a soft spot there matters less. Change the order freely to suit your child's day.

A gentle schedule

One quest per day is plenty. Each quest is also built in parts (usually three), and your child can pause between parts for a stretch or a snack. There's no clock anywhere.

Please read before you start: progress is not saved

These pages keep answers only in the open browser tab, in temporary memory. That means:

  • Short breaks within one sitting are fine — as long as you don't close or refresh the tab.
  • If you refresh, close the tab, or the device sleeps and reloads, that quest starts over from the beginning. Half-finished answers are gone.
  • A results report only appears after the child reaches the end of that quest. There's no way to see partial results, and no way to resume tomorrow where you left off.

So: plan to finish one quest in a single session, and the moment you reach the results page, use its “Print or save these results” button before closing the tab. On a phone or computer you can choose Save as PDF in the print dialog to keep a copy.

Nothing is stored on any server — there is no server. The report exists only in your browser until you close the page, which is exactly why saving it matters.

Running a quest with your child

  • Sit somewhere quiet and ordinary. You stay alongside the whole time.
  • If the word “test” adds pressure, don't use it — it's a quest, a set of puzzles with the cat.
  • Read a question or the directions aloud when that helps. Reading it out is not a hint.
  • During a scored section, don't teach or correct, and don't signal whether an answer was right or wrong. A warm, steady “nice thinking — next one” works for both hits and misses.
  • Encourage effort, not accuracy. “Give it a try” is plenty.
  • Allow breaks the moment they're wanted.
  • Stop if you see fatigue, frustration, or plain guessing. A tired guess is worse than no data — you can finish another day (starting that quest fresh, since progress isn't saved).
  • Afterward, jot down anything that shaped how it went — handwriting, attention, speech, or reading effort. That context matters as much as the score.
  • Hold the result loosely. It's one snapshot of one morning, not the whole child.

Each quest also opens with its own short “For the grown-up” panel with tips specific to that subject — for example, how to rate a word your child reads aloud, or when to move on if they're stuck. Skim it before you tap “Start.”

What to prepare

Most of the work is tapping on screen. A few parts need a pencil or a simple object — and only these, confirmed from each quest itself:

QuestWhat you need
EverythingA phone, tablet, or computer with a web browser, and a way to print or save the final report (the report's button opens your browser's print dialog — choose “Save as PDF” to keep it).
Reading QuestA pencil and paper — used only in Part 3 (the spelling bridge), where you say a word and your child writes it. You tap the ratings.
Math QuestA real ruler and any small object to measure — a book, spoon, or shoe — for the final “Measuring Post” step. No pencil or paper otherwise; fingers and counting out loud are fine.
Word QuestA pencil and paper — used only in the final writing task (one question sentence, then a short 3–4 sentence paragraph about an animal).
Science QuestNothing extra — it's entirely tapping, the shortest of the four.

On the writing and spelling tasks, don't correct spelling or letter formation while your child works — those parts are measuring sentence control and spelling patterns, not handwriting.

Reading the reports

Each report groups the answers by skill area and gives every area a simple label. The labels come from how many were right, not from any outside benchmark:

LabelRoughly meansWhat it suggests
SecureNearly all correct (about 85%+)Owned. Move quickly or skip ahead here.
EmergingMost correct (about 60–84%)Mostly there — a light touch, not a re-teach.
Needs teachingBelow about 60%Worth some direct, parent-supported practice.

The overall plan (Reading, Math & Word)

Those three reports also suggest one of three starting points. In plain terms:

  • Plan A — start Grade 3 now. Foundations are solid; handle any stray miss as it comes up, without a review unit first.
  • Plan B — start Grade 3, with one short strand alongside. Mostly secure, with a single softer area to give a few focused minutes on a few days a week.
  • Plan C — a short repair period first. A few foundations are still shaky and Grade 3 leans on them, so spend two to three weeks on the weakest areas, then begin. This is a brief pause, not a demotion.

The Science report works a little differently — because Grade 3 science moves to new topics, it leans toward “start Grade 3 and revisit anything faded through conversation and everyday looking,” rather than a repair period.

Five things a wrong answer can mean

The reports are built so a miss tells you why, not just that. It helps to sort each one:

  • A concept gap — the idea itself isn't there yet (e.g. “read the digit, not its place,” or “counted the coins, not their values”). Best rebuilt with real objects and talking, not more worksheets.
  • A procedural error — the idea is understood but the written method slipped (e.g. “didn't carry the ten,” “subtracted the smaller from the larger”). A quick method fix, not a concept re-teach.
  • A rusty skill — known before, faded over a break. It usually snaps back with a reminder or two; don't mistake rust for a hole. (In the reading word lists, a “needed a nudge” rating is exactly this.)
  • A Grade 3 preview item — questions clearly marked as previewing material your child hasn't been taught yet. A low score here is normal and expected; it means the course is well-pitched, not that anyone is behind.
  • A low writing score that's really stamina — on the Word Quest, if writing itself was slow or effortful, the grammar underneath may be stronger than the paper shows. Check those skills out loud before concluding they're missing, and let your child demonstrate by talking, pointing, or dictating.

The Reading Quest also asks you one question about speech sounds. It can only notice a pattern, never diagnose anything — if it flags something, it's simply a gentle nudge to mention it at a routine check-up, while you keep teaching reading as usual.

Afterward: one learner profile

Once you've done the quests that make sense for you, look at the reports together rather than one at a time. Four snapshots combine into a single, more honest picture of where your child is. From them you can sketch:

  • Skills that are secure (move quickly or skip)
  • Skills that are emerging (a light touch)
  • Skills that need direct instruction (parent-supported)
  • Curriculum content that can move quickly
  • Areas that need parent support
  • Accommodations that helped (reading aloud, dictating, shorter sessions)
  • A short recheck plan — usually just “work the plan two ordinary weeks, then ask: too easy, about right, or too hard?”

The Combined Results Record is a printable worksheet with a blank space for each of these, so you can hold all four subjects on one page.

One priority at a time. You don't have to act on every miss. Pick the single most useful thing, work it until it's steady, then choose the next. Turning each missed item into its own program is the fastest way to make a calm plan stressful.
